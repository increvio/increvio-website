How Many Calories to Build Muscle? Lean Bulk Without Getting Fat

A big surplus mostly adds fat. Here's how to set a small surplus, how fast the scale should move and when to adjust.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • A small surplus builds about as much muscle as a large one, with less fat gain (Helms et al., 2023).
  • Start with your maintenance calories plus roughly 5 to 15%, more for beginners, less for experienced lifters.
  • Aim to gain about 0.25 to 0.5% of body weight per week as a beginner, less as you get more advanced.
  • Protein of 1.6 to 2.2 g/kg and progressive training matter more than extra calories.

Why a big surplus doesn't build more muscle

Muscle grows slowly. Even under ideal conditions, a beginner might gain a few hundred grams of muscle per month, and an experienced lifter much less. Your body can only use so much extra energy for building muscle. The rest is stored as fat.

In a 2023 study, trained lifters were assigned to a small surplus (about 5% above maintenance) or a large surplus (about 15%) for eight weeks. Muscle gains weren't meaningfully different between groups, but the large-surplus group gained more fat (Helms et al., 2023).

The old "eat everything in sight" bulk mostly leads to a long, frustrating cut afterwards.

Step 1: Estimate your maintenance calories

Your maintenance calories are what you burn per day. A common way to estimate them:

  1. Basal metabolic rate (BMR) with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which is one of the most accurate formulas for most people (Frankenfield et al., 2005): - Men: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5 - Women: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161
  2. Multiply by your daily activity outside of training (about 1.2 for a desk job, up to 1.5 or more for a physical job).
  3. Add your training separately, based on how long and how hard you train.

Any formula is just a starting point. After two to three weeks, your real weight trend tells you your true maintenance.

Step 2: Add a small surplus

ExperienceSurplus above maintenanceTarget gain per week
Beginner (under 1 year)about 15%0.25 to 0.5% of body weight
Intermediate (1 to 3 years)about 10%0.15 to 0.35% of body weight
Experienced (3+ years)about 5 to 6%0.1 to 0.25% of body weight

These ranges follow research recommendations for natural lifters (Iraki et al., 2019). For a 75 kg beginner, that's around 190 to 375 g per week, or roughly 1 to 1.5 kg per month.

Step 3: Set your macros

  • Protein: about 2.0 g per kg of body weight (details here).
  • Fat: about 25 to 30% of calories, and not much below 20%. Very low fat intake can lower testosterone in men (Whittaker & Wu, 2021).
  • Carbohydrates: the rest. Carbs fuel hard training, so don't cut them low while building muscle. At least 3 to 5 g/kg is a good guide.

Step 4: Track your trend and adjust

Weigh yourself several times per week in the morning and look at the weekly average. Then adjust every two to three weeks:

  • Gaining faster than your target? Remove about 100 to 200 kcal per day.
  • Not gaining at all? Add about 100 to 200 kcal per day.
  • Right on target? Keep going.

Small changes, then wait. Daily weight is noisy, so don't react to single weigh-ins.

Should you bulk or lose fat first?

If you're a beginner with a higher body fat level, you may be able to build muscle and lose fat at the same time (often called recomposition), especially with enough protein and good training. Many people feel and look better reducing body fat first, then building muscle from a leaner starting point. Neither is wrong. Pick the goal that motivates you more.

Frequently asked questions

How many calories should I eat to build muscle?

Your maintenance calories plus about 5 to 15%. Beginners can use the higher end, experienced lifters the lower end. Adjust based on your weekly weight trend.

How fast should I gain weight on a bulk?

About 0.25 to 0.5% of body weight per week for beginners, and slower for more experienced lifters. Faster gain is mostly fat.

Can I build muscle without a calorie surplus?

Yes, especially as a beginner, when returning after a break, or with higher body fat. A small surplus makes it easier and faster for most people.
