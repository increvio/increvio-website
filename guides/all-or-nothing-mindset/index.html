All-or-Nothing Mindset: Why It Ruins Your Fitness and How to Stop

Perfect weeks are rare. Good-enough weeks, repeated for months, are what change your body.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • All-or-nothing thinking turns one slip into a full stop: "I ate a cookie, so the day is ruined."
  • Researchers found that strict dieters who believed they had "blown it" ate more afterwards (Polivy & Herman, 1985).
  • Flexible approaches are linked to fewer binges and better long-term results than rigid rules (Westenhoefer et al., 1999).
  • Replace "perfect or nothing" with "most of the time, and never miss twice."

What all-or-nothing thinking looks like

  • "I missed Monday's workout, so I'll start fresh next week."
  • "I already had pizza for lunch, so I might as well order dessert."
  • "I can't do a full hour today, so there's no point going."
  • "I gained a kilo over the holidays. Everything is ruined."

Each thought sounds reasonable in the moment. Each one turns a small dent into a crash.

Why it's so damaging

In classic experiments, psychologists gave dieters a milkshake and told some of them it was high in calories. Those who believed they had broken their diet then ate more ice cream in a later "taste test" than non-dieters did (Herman & Polivy). The thought "the diet is already broken" removed the brakes. This is often called the what-the-hell effect.

The same happens with training. Once the streak, the perfect week or the clean month is broken, the reason to keep going seems to disappear.

The math of "good enough"

Imagine two people over 12 weeks:

Perfect, then quitsGood enough
Weeks 1 to 3100% of plan80% of plan
Weeks 4 to 12Stops after a bad week80% of plan
Total3 good weeks12 good weeks

The "good enough" person does roughly three times more training and on-plan eating. Consistency beats intensity.

How to break the pattern

1. Define a minimum. Decide what counts on a bad day: 20 minutes of training, eating your planned breakfast and lunch, a 30-minute walk. The minimum keeps the chain alive. See short workouts.

2. Use the next-meal rule. After an off-plan meal, go back to your plan at the very next meal. Not tomorrow. Not Monday.

3. Plan flexibility in. Put treats, dinners out and rest days into the plan in advance. A planned pizza isn't a slip. Flexible dieting is linked to fewer binges than rigid dieting (Westenhoefer et al., 1999).

4. Aim for 80 to 90%. Most progress comes from what you do most of the time. A weekly score like "3 of 3 workouts, 17 of 21 meals on plan" is a great week.

5. Never miss twice. One miss is noise. Two in a row is the start of a new pattern. More in Never miss twice.

6. Talk to yourself like a coach. Not "I'm so undisciplined," but "That happens. What's the next step?" Self-compassion after a slip led people to eat less afterwards, not more (Adams & Leary, 2007).

Frequently asked questions

What is all-or-nothing thinking in dieting?

It's the belief that a diet or plan is either followed perfectly or failed completely. One slip then leads to abandoning the plan entirely.

How do I stop the "I already ruined it" feeling?

Go back to the plan at the next meal or session, use a minimum version on bad days, and plan flexible meals in advance so they don't feel like failures.

Is it okay to not follow my plan 100%?

Yes. Following your plan most of the time over many months gives far better results than perfect weeks followed by quitting.
