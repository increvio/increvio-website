Progressive Overload Explained: How to Keep Getting Stronger

If your workouts look the same in three months, your body will too. Here's the simplest way to keep progressing.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • Progressive overload means gradually making your training harder: more weight, more reps or more sets.
  • Adding reps and adding weight both work. In one study, they led to similar muscle and strength gains (Plotkin et al., 2022).
  • The simplest method is double progression: first add reps, then add weight.
  • Write everything down. You can't beat last week if you don't know what you did.

What is progressive overload?

Your body adapts to the demands you put on it. Lift a weight that's challenging, and over time your muscles grow and your nervous system gets better at lifting it. Once it's easy, it's no longer a strong signal to adapt further.

Progressive overload means slowly increasing the challenge so your body keeps adapting. It's the core principle behind every good training program.

Reps or weight?

Both work. A study compared two groups for eight weeks: one group added weight each session, the other added reps at the same weight. Both groups gained similar amounts of muscle and strength (Plotkin et al., 2022).

So you can progress with whichever is practical. Adding reps is especially useful when the next weight jump is big, like going from a 12 kg to a 14 kg dumbbell.

Double progression: the simple method

  1. Pick a rep range, for example 8 to 12 reps.
  2. Start with a weight you can lift for about 8 reps on all sets, stopping one or two reps short of failure.
  3. Each session, try to add a rep or two to some sets.
  4. When you reach 12 reps on all sets, increase the weight by the smallest step available.
  5. With the heavier weight, you'll drop back to around 8 reps. Repeat.

Example on dumbbell press (3 sets, 8 to 12 reps):

WeekWeightReps
120 kg9, 8, 8
220 kg10, 10, 9
320 kg12, 11, 10
420 kg12, 12, 12 (top of range)
522.5 kg9, 8, 8

Only count reps you actually did with good form. If you increase the weight based on what you planned, not what you achieved, you'll stall fast.

How fast can you progress?

  • Beginners can often add weight or reps almost every session for the first months.
  • Intermediates progress more slowly, maybe weekly or every few weeks.
  • Advanced lifters progress over months, often by adding sets or using more advanced programming.

Progress isn't perfectly linear. Bad sleep, stress and a busy week can mean a flat session. Look at the trend over weeks.

When you stall

If a lift doesn't improve for two to three weeks:

  1. Check sleep, food (enough protein and calories?) and stress.
  2. Make sure you're resting long enough between sets (1.5 to 3 minutes for big lifts).
  3. Consider an easier week: half the sets, lighter effort. Fatigue builds up over long training blocks. A lighter week lets it clear.
  4. Swap the exercise for a similar one for a few weeks.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I increase weight?

When you hit the top of your rep range on all sets with good form. For beginners, that might be every week or two per exercise.

What if I can't add weight?

Add reps, add a set, slow the lowering phase, or improve your range of motion. All of these increase the challenge.

Is progressive overload necessary to build muscle?

Yes, in the long run. Without increasing the challenge, your body has no reason to keep adapting.
