How to Start Working Out: A Beginner's Guide That Sticks

Everything a beginner needs, nothing they don't. Plus a simple plan you can start this week.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Start with 2 to 3 full-body sessions per week, about 45 to 60 minutes each.
  • Focus on a handful of basic movements: squat, hinge, push, pull and a core exercise.
  • Leave 1 to 3 reps in reserve at first. Learning the movement matters more than maximum effort.
  • Add weight or reps over time. That's progressive overload, the engine of all progress.
  • Consistency beats intensity. The best beginner plan is the one you do for months.

Why strength training?

Lifting weights isn't only for bodybuilders. Strength training builds muscle and bone, improves blood sugar control, makes everyday life easier and supports healthy aging. A large analysis found that 30 to 60 minutes per week of muscle-strengthening activity was linked to a 10 to 20% lower risk of death from all causes, cardiovascular disease and cancer (Momma et al., 2022).

The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days per week, plus 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity (WHO, 2020).

How often should a beginner train?

Two to three full-body sessions per week is ideal for most beginners. Each muscle gets trained two to three times a week, which works well for growth, and you have rest days in between to recover. See How many days a week should you work out?

A simple 3-day beginner plan

Do this on three non-consecutive days, like Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

Day A

  1. Goblet squat: 3 sets of 8 to 12
  2. Dumbbell bench press: 3 sets of 8 to 12
  3. Lat pulldown: 3 sets of 10 to 12
  4. Romanian deadlift: 2 sets of 10 to 12
  5. Lateral raises: 2 sets of 12 to 15
  6. Plank: 2 × 30 seconds

Day B

  1. Leg press: 3 sets of 10 to 12
  2. Seated cable row: 3 sets of 10 to 12
  3. Shoulder press (dumbbells): 3 sets of 8 to 12
  4. Hip thrust: 3 sets of 10 to 12
  5. Biceps curl and triceps pushdown: 2 sets each of 10 to 15
  6. Dead bug: 2 sets of 10 per side

Alternate A and B: week 1 is A-B-A, week 2 is B-A-B, and so on.

How heavy should you lift?

Pick a weight you can lift for the target reps with good form, finishing each set with about 1 to 3 reps left in the tank. If you could do 10 more reps, it's too light. If your form breaks down, it's too heavy.

Research shows muscles grow across a wide range of reps (roughly 6 to 30) as long as sets are taken reasonably close to failure (Schoenfeld et al., 2017). Beginners don't need to go to failure to grow. See How close to failure should you train?

How to progress

Write down your weights and reps every session. When you hit the top of the rep range for all sets (for example 3 × 12), increase the weight a little next time and work back up. This method is called double progression, and it's all a beginner needs. Read Progressive overload explained.

Rest between sets

Rest about 1.5 to 3 minutes between sets of big exercises and 1 to 2 minutes for small ones. Longer rest lets you lift more, which helps progress (Schoenfeld et al., 2016).

What about cardio?

Cardio is great for your heart and health. Walk daily and add one to three sessions of cycling, running or swimming if you enjoy it. It won't kill your gains. See Cardio and weights for fat loss.

Make it stick

The perfect plan you quit after three weeks is worse than a simple plan you do for a year:

  • Put your training days in your calendar (why it works).
  • Start lighter than you think. Sore for a week is a reason to skip.
  • Get a friend involved (accountability partner).
  • Never miss twice.

What to bring to the gym

  • Water bottle
  • Towel
  • Headphones
  • Your plan (on paper or in an app)
  • Comfortable shoes with a flat, stable sole for lifting

Beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Ego lifting. Too heavy too soon leads to bad form and injuries.
  • Random workouts. Following the same plan for weeks is how you get stronger.
  • Only training the mirror muscles. Legs, back and glutes matter just as much.
  • No rest days. Recovery is when you get stronger.
  • Expecting quick visible changes. Track strength first; looks follow.

Frequently asked questions

How long until I see results from working out?

Strength often improves noticeably within the first two to four weeks as your body learns the movements. Visible muscle changes usually take two to three months of consistent training.

Should beginners use machines or free weights?

Both work. Machines are easier to learn and safe to push hard. Free weights train balance and coordination. A mix is great.

Do I need a personal trainer to start?

No, but a session or two to check your technique can help. Video yourself, start light and focus on controlled movements.

Can I build muscle at home?

Yes. Bodyweight exercises and a pair of adjustable dumbbells are enough for beginners, as long as you progress over time.
