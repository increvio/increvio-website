How to Stick to a Diet: 10 Strategies That Actually Work

Research is clear that sticking to a diet matters more than which diet you choose. Here's how to make sticking to it easier.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • In head-to-head studies, very different diets lead to similar results. Adherence is what predicts success (Dansinger et al., 2005).
  • Plan ahead: people who plan meals tend to eat a more varied, healthier diet (Ducrot et al., 2017).
  • Eat enough protein. It keeps you full and protects muscle.
  • Aim for 80 to 90% on plan, not 100%. One off-plan meal never ruins progress.

The best diet is the one you can stick to

In 2005, researchers randomly assigned 160 adults to four popular diets: low carb, low fat, a points-based program and a balanced "zone" diet. After a year, average weight loss was similar across all four. What mattered most was how well people stuck to the diet they were given, regardless of which one it was (Dansinger et al., 2005).

That's good news. You don't need to find a magic diet. You need an approach that fits your life well enough that you can keep it up for months.

1. Don't cut too hard

Aggressive diets are hard to stick to. Hunger, low energy and cravings build up until something gives. For fat loss, a pace of about 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week is a good target for most people (Helms et al., 2014). Slower is easier to sustain and protects muscle better. See How fast should you lose weight?

2. Eat enough protein

Protein is the most filling macronutrient, and in a calorie deficit it helps you keep muscle. A good target for active people is about 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight per day (Morton et al., 2018). Spread it over three to five meals. More in How much protein per day.

3. Plan your week

Every unplanned meal is a decision made while hungry and tired, which is when decisions are worst. In a study of more than 40,000 French adults, people who planned their meals had better diet quality and were less likely to have obesity (Ducrot et al., 2017).

Spend 20 minutes on the weekend: pick your meals, write a grocery list, and prep a few things. See Weekly meal plan and grocery list.

4. Eat foods you actually like

A diet built on foods you don't enjoy has an expiry date. Keep your favorite foods and adjust portions and frequency instead. Pasta can stay. Pizza can stay. Just plan for them.

5. Use the 80/20 rule

Aim for most meals to be on plan, and leave room for flexibility. Studies on dieting suggest that flexible approaches are linked to fewer binges and less weight regain than rigid, all-or-nothing rules (Westenhoefer et al., 1999). One dinner with friends, a slice of cake at a birthday, a beer on Friday are part of a normal life.

6. Make the default easy

Most of what we eat is habitual. Same breakfast, similar lunches. Use that:

  • Have two or three go-to breakfasts that fit your plan.
  • Keep fruit, yogurt and protein snacks visible; keep snack foods out of sight.
  • Prep lunches for the week on Sunday (meal prep guide).

7. Don't drink your calories

Sugary drinks, juices and fancy coffee drinks add calories without making you full. Switching to water, sparkling water, tea, coffee or zero-calorie drinks is one of the easiest wins.

8. Have a plan for eating out

Restaurants, work events and family dinners are where most diets break. Decide your strategy before you go: a protein-rich main, vegetables, one treat you really want. See Eating out on a diet.

9. Get back on plan at the next meal

The biggest danger isn't the off-plan meal. It's the "day is ruined anyway" thinking afterwards. One heavy meal might add a few hundred calories. A ruined weekend adds thousands. Go back to your plan at the very next meal. See Never miss twice.

10. Track your trend, not every bite

Weigh yourself a few times a week and look at the weekly average. Daily weight swings by one to two kilos because of water and food volume. The trend tells you if your plan works. Adjust calories slightly if the trend doesn't move for two to three weeks.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest diet to stick to?

The one that fits your preferences and schedule. Research shows different diets work similarly when people stick to them, so choose foods you enjoy, eat enough protein and plan ahead.

How do I stop cheating on my diet?

Stop calling it cheating. Plan flexible meals into your week, keep your deficit moderate so hunger stays manageable, and return to your plan at the next meal after any off-plan food.

How long does it take for healthy eating to become a habit?

Habits take around two months on average to feel automatic. Planning and repeating the same meals makes it faster.
