How to Get Motivated to Work Out (and What to Do When You're Not)

Motivation is unreliable. Here's how to create more of it, and how to train anyway on the days it doesn't show up.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Motivation usually comes after you start, not before. Lower the bar to starting.
  • How you feel during a workout predicts whether you'll come back. Make sessions enjoyable enough.
  • Social commitment, visible progress and small rewards create motivation you can count on.
  • On days with zero motivation, rely on a fixed plan and a minimum version, not on how you feel.

The motivation trap

Most people wait to feel motivated, then work out. That works in January. It doesn't work on a rainy Tuesday in March.

Here's the thing: for most people, motivation follows action. You rarely feel like going for a run while sitting on the couch. Ten minutes into the run, you're glad you went. So the goal isn't to feel motivated. The goal is to make starting so easy that you don't need much motivation at all.

1. Shrink the first step

Don't tell yourself "I have to do a full hour of training." Tell yourself "I'll put on my shoes and do the warm-up." If after the warm-up you still want to stop, you're allowed to. You almost never will.

This works because the hardest part of a workout is the transition from not working out to working out. Make the transition tiny.

2. Schedule it like an appointment

People who decide in advance when and where they'll exercise are far more likely to do it. In a classic study, 91% of people who wrote down a specific plan exercised, compared to about a third of people who were only motivated (Milne et al., 2002).

A scheduled workout doesn't need a decision. An unscheduled one needs a new decision every day, and each decision is a chance to say no. Learn how in When-then plans for your workouts.

3. Make it enjoyable enough to come back

A review of 24 studies found that people who felt better during exercise were more likely to be active in the future (Rhodes & Kates, 2015). Pain and misery are not a requirement for results.

  • Choose exercises you don't hate. There are many ways to train every muscle.
  • Leave one or two reps in the tank on most sets. You'll still build muscle (see why).
  • Start lighter than your ego wants. Sore for five days after the first session is the fastest way to skip the second.

4. Bundle it with something you love

Researchers gave gym-goers access to addictive audiobooks that they could only listen to at the gym. Those people went to the gym more often than a control group, at least in the first weeks (Milkman et al., 2014). The trick is called temptation bundling.

Ideas: a podcast you only hear while training, a favorite playlist, a coffee or smoothie you only get after the gym, a series you only watch on the treadmill.

5. Train with someone, or for someone

Knowing someone expects you is one of the strongest motivators. People who joined a program with friends were much more likely to finish it (Wing & Jeffery, 1999).

You don't even need to train together. Sending a friend a check mark after every workout, or competing in a friendly weekly challenge, is enough to make skipping feel worse than going. More in How to find an accountability partner.

6. Make progress visible

Muscle and fat change slowly. If you only look in the mirror, it feels like nothing is happening for weeks. That kills motivation.

Make the small progress visible:

  • Write down your weights. Lifting 2.5 kg more than last month is real, measurable progress.
  • Keep a streak of days or weeks you trained.
  • Use your weight trend (the weekly average), not the daily number.

Monitoring progress has been shown to help people reach their goals, especially when it's written down or shared (Harkin et al., 2016).

7. Use fresh starts

People are more likely to start new goals after "temporal landmarks": the start of a week, a month, a new year, a birthday. Researchers found gym visits jump at these moments (Dai et al., 2014).

You can create these moments yourself. "New week, new plan" every Monday works. Just don't use it as an excuse to wait. Monday is great. Today is better.

8. Remember why, specifically

"Get fit" is too vague to pull you off the couch. Get specific:

  • "I want to carry my kids without my back hurting."
  • "I want to feel confident at the beach in July."
  • "I want to be stronger than I was last year."

Write it down and put it where you'll see it. On hard days, the specific why is stronger than any quote.

9. Remove friction

Every small obstacle is an excuse. Pack your gym bag the night before. Pick a gym that's on your way home. Keep a pair of dumbbells at home for backup. Have a plan written down so you don't need to think at the gym.

10. When all else fails: do the minimum

Some days you won't feel any motivation at all, and that's normal. Don't negotiate with yourself. Do the minimum version: the first two exercises, or 20 minutes. You keep the habit alive, and the minimum often turns into a full session once you've started.

This is the core of the never miss twice rule.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I have no motivation to work out?

Common reasons: the plan is too hard, results feel invisible, workouts feel unpleasant, or you're tired and stressed. Lower the bar, track small wins, and make sure you're sleeping and eating enough.

Is it okay to work out without motivation?

Yes, it's actually the goal. Consistent people train on days they don't feel like it. They rely on schedules and habits, not feelings.

How do I stay motivated long term?

Shift from motivation to systems: fixed days, a plan that progresses, friends who notice, and visible progress. Over about two months, training becomes a habit and needs much less motivation.
