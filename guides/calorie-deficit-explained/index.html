Calorie Deficit Explained: How to Calculate Yours

Every fat loss diet works through a calorie deficit. Here's how to calculate yours and keep it realistic.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways

  • A calorie deficit means eating fewer calories than you burn. It's the common factor behind every diet that leads to fat loss.
  • Estimate maintenance with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation times an activity factor, then adjust using your real weight trend.
  • A deficit for losing about 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week works well for most people.
  • The old "3,500 kcal = 1 pound" rule overestimates long-term loss because the body adapts (Hall et al., 2011).

What is a calorie deficit?

Your body burns energy all day: to keep you alive (basal metabolism), to digest food, to move around and to exercise. If you eat less energy than you burn, your body uses stored energy, mostly fat, to make up the difference. That gap is your calorie deficit.

Low-carb, low-fat, intermittent fasting, points systems: they all work, when they work, because they create a deficit. In head-to-head studies, very different diets lead to similar results when people stick to them (Dansinger et al., 2005).

Step 1: Estimate your basal metabolic rate

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is one of the most accurate for most adults (Frankenfield et al., 2005):

  • Men: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5
  • Women: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

Example: a 30-year-old woman, 70 kg, 168 cm: 10 × 70 + 6.25 × 168 − 5 × 30 − 161 = 700 + 1,050 − 150 − 161 = 1,439 kcal

Step 2: Add daily activity

Multiply by an activity factor for your daily life without training:

Daily lifeFactor
Desk job, little walking1.2
Some walking, on your feet sometimes1.375
On your feet most of the day1.55
Physical job1.725

Our example, desk job: 1,439 × 1.2 = about 1,730 kcal.

Step 3: Add training

Add roughly what your workouts burn, averaged over the week. A 60-minute strength session might burn around 200 to 400 kcal depending on body size and intensity. Three sessions per week at 300 kcal = 900 kcal per week, or about 130 kcal per day.

Maintenance in our example: about 1,860 kcal.

Step 4: Set your deficit

Goal paceApproximate deficit
0.5% of body weight per weekabout 10 to 20% below maintenance
1% of body weight per weekabout 20 to 25% below maintenance

For our example (70 kg), losing 0.35 to 0.5 kg per week means eating about 1,450 to 1,600 kcal.

Guard rails: don't go below about 1,200 kcal (women) or 1,500 kcal (men) without medical supervision, and don't cut more than about 25% below maintenance.

Step 5: Adjust with real data

Formulas are estimates, often off by 10% or more. Your real maintenance shows up in your weight trend:

  1. Eat your target calories consistently for 2 to 3 weeks.
  2. Weigh yourself several times per week and compare weekly averages.
  3. Losing slower than planned? Lower by 100 to 200 kcal. Faster? Add 100 to 200 kcal.

Why the 3,500 kcal rule is too simple

You may have heard that a 500 kcal daily deficit equals 0.45 kg (1 lb) of fat loss per week, forever. In reality, as you lose weight, you burn less: a smaller body needs less energy, and you may move a bit less without noticing. Mathematical models show weight loss slows over time for this reason (Hall et al., 2011). That's normal and why adjusting every few weeks matters. See Weight loss plateau.

Keep muscle while in a deficit

  • Eat about 1.8 to 2.2 g protein per kg of body weight (details).
  • Keep lifting weights (why).
  • Don't go too fast.

Frequently asked questions

How big should my calorie deficit be?

For most people, about 10 to 25% below maintenance, aiming to lose 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week. Bigger deficits are harder to stick to and risk more muscle loss.

Can I lose fat without counting calories?

Yes, if you create a deficit another way, for example with a meal plan, portion control or eating mostly whole foods. Counting is one tool, not a requirement.

Why am I not losing weight in a calorie deficit?

Often the deficit is smaller than estimated (untracked food, overestimated activity), or water retention is hiding fat loss. Track the weekly average for two to three weeks and adjust.
