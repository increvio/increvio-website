Muscle Soreness (DOMS): Should You Train When You're Sore?

Sore muscles are normal, especially at the start. But soreness isn't a measure of a good workout.

By Timur Leontyev, Founder of Increvio
Updated November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

Key takeaways

  • DOMS (delayed onset muscle soreness) peaks about 24 to 72 hours after unfamiliar or hard exercise (Cheung et al., 2003).
  • It's strongest after new exercises and lowering phases (eccentrics), and drops quickly with repeated training: the repeated bout effect (McHugh, 2003).
  • Soreness is not a reliable sign of muscle growth (Schoenfeld & Contreras, 2013).
  • Light activity and normal training of other muscles are fine. Sharp pain, swelling or pain that gets worse is different: rest and get it checked.

What causes muscle soreness?

DOMS is linked to small disruptions in muscle fibers and the surrounding tissue, plus the inflammation and repair processes that follow. It's not caused by lactic acid, which clears within an hour after training.

You'll feel it most:

  • When you start training or come back after a break
  • After new exercises or a new program
  • After lots of eccentric work: lowering weights slowly, walking downhill, Romanian deadlifts, lunges

Does soreness mean the workout worked?

No. A review concluded that soreness is a poor indicator of muscle growth or training quality (Schoenfeld & Contreras, 2013). Experienced lifters often build muscle with little soreness, because their muscles have adapted to the exercises.

Chasing soreness can even backfire: being very sore makes you skip the next session, and consistency beats intensity.

The repeated bout effect

The first time you do a new exercise, you might be sore for days. Do the same exercise a week later, and soreness is much milder. This protective adaptation is called the repeated bout effect (McHugh, 2003). It's why your first two weeks feel the worst.

Should you train when you're sore?

  • Mild to moderate soreness: yes. Train other muscles, or train the sore muscle with a proper warm-up. Soreness often decreases once you're warm.
  • Very sore, reduced range of motion: train other muscle groups or do light cardio, and give the sore muscle another day.
  • Sharp pain, swelling, bruising, dark urine or pain in a joint: that's not normal DOMS. Rest and see a doctor.

How to avoid extreme soreness

  • Start lighter than you think in a new program, leaving 2 to 3 reps in reserve.
  • Increase volume gradually over the first two weeks.
  • Keep exercises consistent for a few weeks instead of changing every session.
  • Warm up properly (how).

Frequently asked questions

How long does muscle soreness last?

Usually 2 to 5 days, peaking around 1 to 3 days after training. It's typically worst in your first weeks.

Is it bad to not be sore after a workout?

No. Lack of soreness doesn't mean you didn't build muscle. Track your lifts instead.

Should I stretch to prevent soreness?

Stretching before or after training doesn't meaningfully reduce soreness. Light movement and gradual progression help more.
